Positions are in source pixel space — native MSX playfield, origin top-left, +x right / +y down. The ball is addressed by the top-left of its visible sprite, never its centre. The demo renders this space at 3× on a cropped 208×124 canvas; the physics never sees the scale.

01

Background

Environment and geometry every later rule is measured against. Bricks are 16 px wide × 8 px tall, laid out 11 columns × 12 rows from grid origin (16, 24). The playfield is bounded by three real walls plus a lost line.

02

Motion

Direction is not a velocity vector. It is a single signed index, skewness ∈ {±1 … ±8}, and the per-move velocity (ySpeed, xSpeed) is derived from it each move. The sign sets vertical direction (+ up, − down); the magnitude sweeps the angle from shallow to steep.

Speed — rate-limited, not per-pixel

Magnitude is decoupled from direction. The ball moves on a schedule set by two derived values: moveTarget — it moves only once every moveTarget frames — and speedMultiplier — on a move frame it applies the velocity vector speedMultiplier + 1 times (so even mult = 0 moves once). Both come from one lookup indexed by speedPos (0–15), the table chosen by |skewness| (_1 for |s| ∈ {3,6}, else _2).

Design intent · why two tables

|s| = 3 and 6 are the only pure 45° diagonals — vectors (±1, ±1), √2 ≈ 1.41 px per step. Every other magnitude is a 2:1 vector ((±2, ±1) or (±1, ±2)), √5 ≈ 2.24 px per step. A diagonal step covers less ground, so table _1 is tuned with smaller moveTargets to compensate — keeping the ball's on-screen speed roughly constant whichever way it heads. The split isn't about the angle; it's the two per-step distance families (√2 vs √5).

The source doesn't comment it, but the arithmetic confirms the intent — effective speed = (mult + 1) × |vector| ÷ moveTarget, in px per frame:

_1 vs _2 · effective on-screen speed

15[2,1] → 4.24[1,1] → 4.47+5%

Within ~5% at every step — the diagonal and 2:1 trajectories feel like one speed.

Acceleration

speedPos starts at 12 and climbs on every bounce, capped at 15. Each bounce increments a counter; when it exceeds BALL_SPEED_TABLE[speedPos], speedPos advances and the counter resets. Early steps are one bounce apart; the top steps take many — 9 bounces to leave speedPos 12, 17 to leave 13 — so the ball ramps up quickly, then plateaus near the cap.

Worked example · initial state

speedPos 12, skewness 1 → table _2, [1, 3]; velocity (−1, +2). The ball moves every 3rd frame, and on that frame applies (−1, +2) twice → steps up-right by (−2, +4) px, then holds two frames. As bounces accumulate, the same skewness moves more often and further per move.

03

Wall collision

Walls are checked once per frame, after the whole move loop — not per sub-step. Each check is gated on the relevant speed's sign, so a wall only fires while the ball heads into it.

Response

On a hit, in order: accelerate (the bounce counts toward speedPos) → flip the speed axis and reflect skewness to match → clamp to the face → and a separate counter perturbs the angle every 40th wall bounce, so a long rally slowly wanders off a repeating path.

The reflect-skewness step keeps the stored angle consistent with the flipped velocity — a shallow up-right ball (s = 1) off the right wall becomes a shallow up-left ball (9 − 1 = 8). The sign-gating is load-bearing: it is exactly why a brick/border bounce that has already flipped a speed axis is not re-bounced by the wall check that frame (§4).

04

Brick collision

Bricks are checked per unit sub-step — after every single application of the velocity vector, inside the move loop. This is the anti-tunnelling guarantee: one sub-step moves the ball at most ±2 px on an axis, and a brick is 8 px tall, so no sub-step can skip one. The loop does not stop after a hit (for normal bricks), so a fast ball can hit several bricks in a frame.

The routine uses a leading contact edge of the ball — which edge depends on the movement direction — and converts it to a grid cell. It computes two cells: CURR from the ball's position and PREV from position − speed. Comparing them classifies the crossing and selects the face to test and the axis to bounce.

Why those offsets — leading edge + grid frame

Each offset does two jobs at once: it selects the ball's leading edge — the side facing the way it is moving — and shifts that edge into the brick grid's pixel frame (origin (16, 24), §1). Moving up, the edge that touches a brick first is the ball's top; the top is the ball's own y, and the grid's Y origin is 24 — so y − 24 lands the top edge on the right row. The other three faces are the same idea rotated.

The giveaway is the arithmetic: the two offsets on each axis differ by exactly 5 — the ball's collision size (24 − 19 vertical, 17 − 12 horizontal). The larger offset picks the near edge (top / left); subtracting the 5 px ball reaches the far edge (bottom / right). So whichever way the ball travels, the edge tested is the one that actually makes contact. (Vertically the base is exactly the grid origin, 24; horizontally it sits one pixel off the render origin — a small collision-vs-render bias baked into the original.)

Guards — is the cell even on the board?

Turning a pixel into a cell index means the next step is an array lookup into a fixed 11×12 brick map. But the ball roams the whole playfield — above the bricks, down by the paddle, against the walls — and most of it is not brick territory, so those positions convert to indices outside the grid. On the original Z80, querying the brick bitmap out of range reads the bytes past the table: the ball would "bounce off nothing" (a phantom brick from garbage) or read into unrelated state. The guards are range checks that reject off-grid cells before the lookup, and double as an early-out when there is simply no brick to hit.

One detail makes them elegant: the math is unsigned 8-bit (the port's & 0xFF reproduces it). A ball above the field makes y − 24 go negative, which wraps to a large byte — e.g. y = 5 → (5 − 24) & 255 = 237 → row 29. A ball below the field gives an honest 12+. Both land ≥ 12, so a single upper-bound check rejects the ball being above OR below the field — underflow wraps high, so there is no separate "< 0" case to test.

Why the order matters: the border and double-impact specials fire on indices that are deliberately off the normal grid — curr col = 11 / 15 at a wall, prev row = 12 one row below the field. A pure top-of-function bounds check (as an early build had) would reject those before their special path ran. So the two "just off the edge" cases are handled between the guards, and guard D (curr col ≥ 11) sits after the border check, not before it. The 13 in guard B vs the 12 in E is the same trick: prev row = 12 must survive B to reach the double-impact, then E rejects it for the ordinary classification.

The ↯ rows — border and double impact — hand off to a special path instead of the ordinary classification below. Both handle the ball at the field's outer edge, where its previous cell is off the grid and the normal classification (which needs both cells in-field) would throw it out. The border special covers the side edges; double impact the top and bottom.

The wall-adjacent border case is subtler, and its numbers are easy to misread. The grid is columns 0–10; the side walls sit just outside it. When the ball's leading edge crosses past the last column, the cell math lands on curr col = 11 at the right wall — or, by the same byte-wrap the guards use, curr col = 15 at the left — and those indices are the wall, not a brick. Yet the ball's body still overlaps the last brick column (prev col = 10 on the right, 0 on the left), so it straddles the corner: leading edge in the wall, still touching the edge brick. When it is also crossing into a neighbouring row, this special decides whether the trajectory catches that edge brick's face (bounce off the brick) or slips past into the wall (wall bounce).

Wall-adjacent border · the ball straddles the last column and the wall

edge brick (prev col 10 / 0) wall = curr col 11 (right) / 15 (left) ball, straddling

Double impact is that same idea at the top and bottom edges — and it's the direct edge cousin of the ambiguous corner. The ball's contact point steps from just outside the field into the edge row: moving up, prev row 12 → curr row 11 (into the bottom row from below); moving down, prev row 31 → curr row 0 (into the top row from above — the 31 is the byte-wrap of "above the field", the guards' trick again). Crossing in diagonally, the ball faces two candidate bricks at once — a brick's bottom/top face (vertical bounce) or a neighbour's side (horizontal bounce) — and the routine runs the same RESOLVE_ CORNER sub-step machinery to decide which. It also reads the column the previous sub-step's hit left, so a fast ball that strikes a second brick as it enters the edge in one frame is resolved. Hence "double".

The last row — an ambiguous corner (Δrow & Δcol) — is resolved by sub-stepping along an auxiliary slope, counting TICKS_TO_HIT until the trajectory crosses the brick's Y edge, then reconstructing the exact X there to decide which face actually got hit — the same machinery that pins the impact pixel for the snap.

Response

When a face test finds a present brick: snap the ball flush to the exact hit pixel (so it never visibly overlaps) → bounce with the same vertical/horizontal primitives as the walls → apply the effect: updateSpeed() to accelerate, then a per-type dispatch. The demo's blocks are all unbreakable, so the only action is a shared counter that perturbs the angle every 20th hit — keeping the ball lively in a box that never empties. No brick removed, no score, no capsule.

The faithful wall-poke

One border sub-case (ball at the wall, same row) does a horizontal bounce without snapping — its snap was computed by COMPUTE_PRECISE_HIT_POINT, whose result is a dead vestige in the source (unread at both call sites). So on a fast ball the ball sits up to ~3 px past the wall face — x reaches 15 (left) / 189 (right) — for the single frame before the flipped xSpeed carries it back. This is faithful, not a bug: the sign-gated wall clamps don't re-fire, so there is no double-bounce and the ball always recovers.

Verified — 0 escapes over 20 000 frames

The headless self-test fires the ball into the enclosure for tens of thousands of frames (normal and accelerated to speedPos 15) and asserts x ∈ [15, 189] and y never reaches the lost line 184 (the brick floor holds it). Measured: 0 escapes, 0 losses, the ball exercising all 16 skewness values via the perturbation.
